01 / UNDERSTAND

It's not always "picky eating."

Eating involves sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, expectations and feelings. A difficult mealtime may be a sign that a child needs an adjustment, not pressure.

🧩

Autism

Some autistic children experience food textures, smells, temperatures, or changes in routine more intensely. Predictability, familiar foods, and sensory adjustments may help.

Needs vary from child to child.
🌟

ADHD

Some children with ADHD become distracted, miss hunger cues, have difficulty staying at the table, or experience appetite changes with certain medications.

Ask a clinician about medication-related concerns.
💛

Beyond labels

A child can have sensory food differences with either condition, both, or neither. Every child deserves support shaped around their own needs and health.

Observe the child, not just a diagnosis.
💬

Try this shift: Instead of asking “Why won't you just eat it?” ask “What feels difficult about this food or this moment?”

02 / THE SENSORY EXPERIENCE

See food through their senses.

Select a sense to explore what a child might experience and how a caregiver can respond. No two children have the same sensory profile.

WHAT IT MIGHT FEEL LIKE

Texture can be overwhelming.

Crunchy, mushy, sticky and mixed textures can feel very different inside the mouth.

Supportive idea

Keep a reliable familiar food available. Offer a separate option or a small, no-pressure way to explore another texture.

Sensory differences are not misbehavior. If you notice frequent choking, coughing, painful swallowing, or major nutritional concerns, speak with a healthcare professional rather than relying on sensory adjustments alone.

03 / CULTURE & COMMUNITY

Culture is part of care.

Inclusive feeding support respects a family's traditions, language, values, and the child's comfort. The goal is participation—not making every child eat alike.

A COMMUNITY THAT MAKES SPACE

One table.
Many traditions.
Everyone welcome.

From home-cooked family meals to school lunchboxes, celebrations, and shared community gatherings, food tells many stories. Sensory accommodations can sit alongside cultural identity.

04 / PRACTICAL SUPPORT

Small changes, calmer meals.

These ideas can help children feel safer and more understood, but they are not a substitute for an individualized assessment when nutrition or health is affected.

🕊️01

Reduce pressure

Skip bribes, shaming, or forcing bites. Invite curiosity without requiring tasting, touching or finishing a plate.

📅02

Make it predictable

Explain what will be served, where everyone will sit, and when the meal begins. A simple picture schedule may help.

🥣03

Offer a familiar option

Include a reliably accepted food when possible and let new foods stay separate. Food exploration can happen at the child's pace.

🌤️04

Adjust the setting

Consider lighting, smells, noise, seat position, utensils and the number of people at the table.

⏰05

Notice appetite patterns

ADHD-related distraction or medication may affect hunger. Discuss persistent appetite concerns or medication changes with the prescriber.

🤍06

Celebrate connection

Success may mean sitting nearby, staying calm, sharing a story or communicating a need—not necessarily eating a new food.

    06 / KNOW WHEN TO ASK

    When does a child need more support?

    Some selective eating is common in childhood. But significant distress, nutrition concerns or unsafe swallowing should be assessed rather than managed with reassurance alone.

    🩺

    Speak with a pediatrician if...

    • Foods are very limited or the variety is shrinking.
    • Meals cause persistent distress or disrupt family, school or daily life.
    • You notice concerns about growth, energy, dehydration, constipation or possible nutrient deficiencies.
    • Food refusal may relate to pain, reflux, allergies, oral-motor or other medical issues.
    • Medication seems to change your child's appetite.

    A pediatrician may recommend a dietitian, occupational therapist, speech-language/feeding specialist, or mental-health professional when appropriate.

    ⚠️

    Get urgent medical help if...

    • A child is choking or having trouble breathing.
    • Swallowing causes repeated choking/coughing, or there is concern about aspiration.
    • A child is unable to drink, shows signs of significant dehydration, or is suddenly very unwell.

    Do not attempt home feeding exercises for a suspected swallowing problem without professional assessment.

    What about ARFID?

    Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder can involve sensory sensitivities, low interest in eating, or fear of consequences such as choking. It is not simply a synonym for picky eating, autism or ADHD. Diagnosis requires professional assessment of the impact.

    07 / COMMON QUESTIONS

    A few questions parents ask.

    Clear answers without blame, food rules, or one-size-fits-all expectations.

    Does selective eating mean my child has autism, ADHD or ARFID?＋

    No. Children can be selective for many reasons, including development, medical concerns and sensory preferences. Autism and ADHD may be associated with feeding difficulties, but eating patterns alone cannot diagnose a condition. ARFID is assessed by a qualified clinician when restriction has a significant impact.

    Should I insist my child tries every food at a cultural celebration?＋

    No. A child can participate by greeting relatives, sitting with family, helping with preparations or enjoying a familiar food. A celebration should not become a public test of food tolerance.

    Can we keep our cultural foods when making sensory adjustments?＋

    Absolutely. Ask which family foods are important and which elements feel comfortable. For example, you might keep sauces separate or offer a familiar texture—but the right accommodation depends on the child, not their cultural identity.

    Is there a special diet that cures autism or ADHD?＋

    No specific food or elimination diet cures autism or ADHD. Restrictive diets can cause nutritional problems and should not be started as a treatment without individualized medical and dietetic advice.

    What should I tell my child's school or nursery?＋

    Share the child's reliable foods, sensory triggers, allergies or medical needs, cultural and dietary requirements, mealtime routine, communication preferences, and what adults should do if the child becomes distressed. Ask the school not to shame or publicly compare lunches.
